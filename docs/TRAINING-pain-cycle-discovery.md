# YLL Deep-Dive #1: The Pain Cycle (Discovery That Actually Sells)

> Written 2026-08-21. The deepest-value area from Hormozi's course for your business, because it's
> your biggest proven gap. Across your real calls, reps name the customer's driver back to them only
> **2.3%** of the time, and discovery scores about **26%** even on your real 2-minute-plus
> conversations. This is where the money is leaking. This doc turns "understand the pain" into
> something your team can drill on purpose.

## The one idea
People don't buy lights. They buy a feeling and they buy an escape from a pain. Your job on the call
is to find the pain, bring it to the top of their mind, and quantify what it's costing them. Hormozi's
line: **the pain is the pitch.** Spend most of the call here. The pitch itself is only two minutes.

Why it works: being deprived of something creates the motivation to fix it. When you make the ladder,
the dead strands, the January takedown, or the surprise fees feel real and present, you create the
urgency that makes them act. You are not inventing pain. You are reminding them of pain they already
have, so they finally do something about it.

## The three steps (this is the C-L-O of the CLOSER framework)

### Step 1: Clarify (get them to say the problem out loud)
- "What made you want to do lights this year?"
- "What's got you looking at this now, versus last year?"
Listen for the real driver: a new house, a kid's first Christmas, hosting a party, tired of doing it
themselves, keeping up with the block, getting older and off the ladder.

### Step 2: Overview the past pain (ask what they've tried, then quantify it)
This is the part your calls skip. Ask, then dig with numbers.

**The opening pain question (per line of business):**
- Holiday: "Have you done lights before, yourself or with a company? How did that go?"
- Permanent: "What's got you looking at permanent, the seasonal hassle or the year-round look?"
- Rebook (past customer): "Thinking back to last season, what did you love, and what would you change?"

**Then quantify the cost. Pick the ones that fit what they said:**
- Time: "How many weekends did putting them up eat? And taking them down in January?"
- Danger: "Were you up on the ladder yourself? How high does your roofline get?"
- Reliability: "Did any of it burn out mid-season? What happened when it did?"
- Money creep: "Did the final bill match the quote, or did takedown and storage get tacked on?"
- Storage: "And where does all of it live the other ten months, the garage, the attic?"
- Status/feeling: "How did the house end up looking compared to what you pictured?"

The goal is that they hear themselves describe the hassle and the letdown. That is the motivation.

### Step 3: Label it back (say it out loud: this is your 2.3% behavior)
Feed back where they are, where they want to be, and what's been in the way. When you nail it, they
say "yeah, exactly," and that is the moment they're sold, before you've pitched anything.
- Busy: "So it sounds like you're just done being the one up on the ladder every December."
- Excited: "So you want the house people slow down for, and last year didn't get there."
- Guarded: "So last time you got burned, and you want to know it'll actually get handled this time."
- Status: "So you want yours to be the best-looking house on the block, done right."

Then, and only then, you bridge into the pitch: "Here's the good news, that's exactly what we fix..."

## The four common mistakes (all of these are in your real calls)
1. Jumping to price before any pain is surfaced. (Your #1 leak.)
2. Asking "have you done this before" but not quantifying what it cost them. A question without the
   follow-up numbers does nothing.
3. Never labeling it back, so the customer never feels understood.
4. Talking more than the customer. Aim for them talking two-thirds of the time. Ask, then be quiet.

## THE DRILL (how to actually practice this)

Discovery is a skill, and skills are drilled, not read. Run this as a 10-minute daily huddle.

### Format (Hormozi's method: one thing, fast feedback, repeat)
1. Pick ONE skill for the week. Week one: "quantify the pain." Not five things, one.
2. One person plays the rep, one plays the homeowner. The coach watches for the one skill only.
3. As they go, the coach gives fast, real-time cues: "good, keep going," or "pause, try that again."
   Do not save all feedback for the end. One correction at a time.
4. When the rep finally does it right, have them do it right **five more times in a row** so it sticks.
5. Rotate. Everyone reps it, including you (player-coach goes first, shares their own misses).

### The four homeowner personas to practice against
Run each rep through all four so they learn to read and match:
- **The Busy One:** "I just don't have time to deal with it this year." (Wants done-for-you.)
- **The Excited One:** "I want it to look amazing, we're hosting this year." (Wants the vision.)
- **The Guarded One:** "The last company left half my house dark in December." (Wants proof + guarantee.)
- **The Status One:** "I want the best-looking house on the street." (Wants premium, standout.)

### What a 10-out-of-10 rep does (the scorecard for the drill)
- Asked at least 3 real questions before any price.
- Asked what they'd done before AND quantified the cost (a number, a weekend, a specific letdown).
- Named the persona back out loud and got a "yeah, exactly."
- Talked less than the homeowner.
This maps to the emotional-diagnosis dimension your copilot already grades, so the drill and the real
call are scored the same way.

### Practice against Claude
Between huddles, a rep can practice solo: open Claude and say "play a guarded homeowner calling Yule
Love Lights about holiday lights, raise the objections a real one would, and score me on whether I
found and named your pain." It plays the customer, then grades the run on this same rubric.

## Where to go even deeper later
- Build the objection library from your real transcripts, in the customers' own words, so the personas
  above sound exactly like your actual callers.
- Add a "cost of inaction" line to the quote itself (what another season of DIY costs in time and risk),
  so the pain is on paper, not just on the call.
